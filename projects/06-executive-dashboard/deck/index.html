The problem

Why executives stop looking at dashboards

  • Everything moves every week. Weekly retail numbers swing by a few per cent for no reason at all. A page that colours those swings red and green is reporting noise in two colours.
  • Seasonality is reported as news. Garden furniture falls off a cliff every autumn. On a quarter-over-quarter view that looks like a crisis.
  • No one owns a number. Forty tiles, no names, no decisions. The meeting becomes a tour of the page.
  • It only looks backwards. The models that predict what happens next live somewhere else entirely.

Design

Four rules, applied to every tile on the page

1. A metric needs an owner and a decisionEach of the ten metrics is on the page because a named person uses it to decide something. "Realised price" exists so merchandising can see whether the discount plan is holding, not because it is available.
2. Signal before sizeEvery number is compared with what it should have been for that week of the year, not with last week. The page says "within normal" or flags it, before it says how big the change was.
3. Sentences, not charts, at the topThe first thing on the page is a ranked list of what needs attention, written in plain English with the owner attached. Charts are for the person who wants to check.
4. The models report here tooChurn risk, acquisition value, persona coverage, forecast accuracy and media return appear on the same page, each with the action it implies.

The method

Every metric gets a baseline, and the baseline never sees the week it judges

Shaded band: three standard deviations around the expected level. Amber points: weeks the control rules flagged.

For each metric and each week, a model is fitted on everything before that week only: a trend, an annual shape, the holiday peak, back-to-school and flyer weeks. It produces an expected level and a measure of how much a normal week varies around it.

Three rules then decide whether to speak up, borrowed from a century of factory process control:

  • a week beyond three standard deviations
  • two of three weeks beyond two
  • eight weeks in a row on the same side of the baseline
The third rule is the one that earns its keep. A drift of half a standard deviation never trips an eyeball, and it is exactly how a problem arrives.

The alert engine

Nine sentences, ranked, each with a name on it

Items come from four places: a control rule firing, a quarter-on-quarter change larger than normal variation, a slice behaving unusually for the season, and the models.

Each is scored on how far it is from normal and how much money sits behind it, then sorted. Severity is a colour, not a word count.

  • High: beyond three standard deviations
  • Medium: a pattern, or a real change in the wrong direction
  • Low: a real change in the right direction
  • Green: something that looks alarming and is not
The list is capped by the data, not by a target length. A quiet week produces a short list, which is the correct output for a quiet week.

Honest limits

What this page cannot do

  • It cannot tell you why. A control signal says a metric left its normal range. The cause is a separate question, and usually needs an experiment rather than another chart.
  • It cannot prove a campaign worked. Sales rose and marketing spent money. Only a holdout settles which caused which, which is why projects 1 and 5 are built around them.
  • The baseline is only as good as the history. A metric needs a year before it can be judged, and a genuine step change will be called a signal for several weeks before the baseline catches up. That is the correct behaviour, but it has to be explained once.
  • Three rules on ten metrics will produce false alarms. Roughly one week in a hundred will trip a rule by chance. With ten metrics that is about one spurious flag a quarter, and the page is better for accepting it.
  • Nothing here replaces a human. The alert list is an agenda, not a decision. Its purpose is to make sure the meeting starts on the two things that matter rather than the first chart on the page.
Governance: every metric has one definition, written in SQL in one place. If the dashboard and the finance pack disagree, one of them is not using this mart.

Appendix

Method, definitions and limits

Data. One weekly fact table built from every order line, plus the media plan, the trading calendar and service records. 156 weeks to . Slices by category, channel and province reconcile to the company total to the dollar.

Baseline. For each metric and week, a linear model on trend, three annual harmonics, holiday peak, back-to-school and flyer weeks, fitted only on earlier weeks. Metrics need weeks of history before they are judged.

Control rules. Three standard deviations; two of three beyond two; eight consecutive weeks on one side.

Period comparison. The average of the last N weeks against the N before, with a standard error from the week-to-week variation of both. "Real" means the difference exceeds two standard errors.

Decomposition. Revenue is split into customers, orders per customer and average basket on a log scale, so the three parts add to the total change.

New customers are counted in the week they joined the loyalty programme, not the week of their first order: the latter puts every pre-existing member into the first week of the extract and invents an acquisition spike.

Limits. No competitor, weather or macroeconomic inputs exist in this dataset. The baseline treats a step change as a run of signals until it has a year of new history. False alarm rate is roughly one metric-week in a hundred.

Synthetic data. Maple & Main is fictional. Figures illustrate the method, not any real company.

Code. SQL, Python and SAS, with a metric dictionary and QC log, are in the project repository.

Method selection

No single method wins everywhere, and the most complex one wins nowhere

MethodCompanyCategoryStoreStore x category

Weighted absolute percentage error. The best figure in each column is highlighted.

  • At company and category level, last year's shape with the level reset to the last eight weeks is hard to beat. Three years of history is only two full seasonal cycles, and a regression cannot learn the holiday peak better than simply looking at it.
  • At store and store-by-category level, the regression wins: individual series are too noisy for last year's actual to be a reliable guide.
  • The boosted model is worst at every level. With 150 weekly observations per series there is nothing for it to learn that the simpler models have not already taken.

Lead time

Thirteen weeks out is barely worse than one week out

Error by weeks ahead, company level

Best method against the benchmark that only knows where demand was last week.

Demand here is driven by things known months in advance: the season, the holiday peak, the flyer schedule. A forecast built only from those does not decay as the horizon lengthens.

A forecast built from recent demand gains nothing from being made late either: repeating last week is about times worse than the best method at every horizon, one week out and thirteen.

This is the practical point for planning. Buying decisions made a quarter ahead are not penalised by being made early, so the lead time is a supply-chain constraint, not a forecasting one.

Hierarchy

Forecast each level directly. Do not split and do not stack.

Category-level error, by how the forecast is produced

Measured on the five forecast origins held back from fitting the ranges.

  • Splitting the company forecast by last year's category shares is times worse. Categories peak at different times of year, and a single share flattens that.
  • Adding up store-by-category forecasts is better but still worse than forecasting categories directly: the noise in 480 thin series does not fully cancel.
  • Direct forecasts disagree with each other, so a reconciliation step is still needed before the numbers are published. Reconcile the published figures; do not let one level generate another.

The decision

Ordering to the forecast is a mistake

Cost of a week's ordering, by service level

Lost margin plus markdown across 480 store-and-category combinations, in CAD. Demand is measured as revenue at full price.

A unit short costs the margin it would have earned. A unit over costs the markdown needed to clear it. Being short hurts more, so the right order is above the forecast, at the point of its range.

Ordering ruleCost a yearFill rate
Total cost of forecast uncertainty is a year, or of revenue at this level. Halving the remaining error would be worth a year, which is the budget any further forecasting work has to earn.

Four actions

1
Run a method per level, not a model for everythingLast year adjusted to the current level at company and category; the calendar regression at store and store-by-category. Re-run the comparison every year; the winner can change.

2
Order to the service level, not to the forecastSet order quantities at the point of the forecast range. It is a one-line change to the ordering rule and needs no new model.
a year
3
Publish the range with every numberA company forecast is good to within a few points; a store and category forecast can be a third out either way. Planners who see only the midpoint will treat the two as equally firm.
Prevents false confidence
4
Spend the next effort on the trading calendar, not the algorithmKnowing the promotion plan earlier and more precisely beat every modelling change tested here. A cleaner events calendar is worth more than a better model.

Appendix

Method, assumptions and limits

Data. 156 complete Monday weeks, from 6 November 2023 to 1 November 2026, aggregated from every order line. Store-level series count in-store sales only.

Backtest. Ten origins four weeks apart from week 104, each forecasting thirteen weeks. Every model is refitted at every origin on data available at that point.

Methods. Three benchmarks; ridge regression on log demand with trend, five Fourier harmonics, trading-calendar flags and media spend; a boosted model pooled across series; and a simple average of the two best.

Ranges. Empirical quantiles of actual divided by forecast, estimated on the first five origins and tested on the last five.

Assumed, not measured. A gross margin and a markdown to clear overstock. Both are adjustable in the explorer, and the optimal service level moves with them.

Limits. The ordering model treats each week independently: there is no inventory carried between weeks, no lead time and no minimum order quantity. A real replenishment system would soften both the shortfalls and the markdowns, so the cost figures are an upper bound on the benefit of accuracy.

Not tested. Price elasticity and cannibalisation between categories. Demand is forecast as it happened under the actual price and promotion plan, not as a response to a changed one.

Synthetic data. Maple & Main is fictional. Figures illustrate the method, not any real company.

Code. SQL, Python and SAS, with a model card and QC log, are in the project repository.
